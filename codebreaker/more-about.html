More about the game

Bulls and Cows looks like a guessing game, but every answer is a clue that removes possibilities. This page explains the maths behind it and a strategy you can actually use.

1. The rules in one minute

The computer hides a password made of different digits. You type a guess of the same length. Each guess is scored:

  • Bull — the digit is correct and in the correct position.
  • Cow — the digit is in the password but in the wrong position.

Example: if the password is 4 2 7 1 and you guess 1 2 3 4, the 2 is a bull, while the 1 and 4 are cows. The 3 is not in the password at all. Score: 1 bull, 2 cows.

2. How many passwords are possible?

Digits may not repeat, so the number of passwords is the number of ways to choose an ordered list of n different digits from 0–9:

3 digits: 10 × 9 × 8 = 720
4 digits: 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5,040
5 digits: 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 × 6 = 30,240

“Average lower bound” comes from information theory. It is the absolute best any strategy could do on average — real strategies need more guesses. For 4 digits the best known strategy averages 5.21 guesses and never needs more than 7.

3. Information and “surprise”, without the jargon

Before your first guess there are 5,040 possible 4-digit passwords. After an answer, only some of them still fit. The game shows this as possible passwords left.

In information theory, the amount of information in an answer is measured in bits. One bit is the information you get from a fair coin toss: it halves the possibilities. If an answer removes a lot of passwords, it gives you more bits and you are closer to the secret.

information = log₂(number of possibilities)

For 4 digits, log₂(5040) ≈ 12.30 bits. A single answer can have at most 14 different bull/cow scores (the pair “3 bulls, 1 cow” is impossible), so one answer can carry at most log₂(14) ≈ 3.81 bits. Dividing the total by the maximum gives the lower bound:

12.30 ÷ 3.81 ≈ 3.23 guesses on average

That is why the lower bound is not the whole story: an answer rarely splits the possibilities perfectly evenly. A good player tries to make the next answer as surprising as possible by choosing a guess whose possible scores leave groups of similar size.

4. A strategy that works

  1. Open with different digits. For 4 digits, 0123 is a popular opening because it tests four different digits and positions. The optimal computer strategy found by Tetsuro Tanaka also starts with 0123.
  2. Keep only passwords that fit. After every answer, cross out every password that would have produced a different number of bulls or cows. The “possible left” counter does this for you.
  3. Choose an informative guess. Prefer a guess that splits the remaining passwords into groups of similar size. You want the next answer to surprise you, not to confirm what you already know.
  4. Break ties with a real candidate. If two guesses would split the set equally well, choose one that could actually be the password, so you might win immediately.
  5. Use the bulls and cows together. Two bulls narrows the position of two digits; cows tell you which other digits are present but misplaced. Do not read one without the other.
  6. Track repeated digits. A digit can appear in at most one position, so once a digit is a bull, it cannot be a cow elsewhere.

On 5 digits the search space is six times larger than on 4, but each answer can also have up to 20 outcomes, so a good question can still remove a large chunk of possibilities.

5. Known results and further reading

The 4-digit game is the classic version. An optimal strategy solves every 5,040-password game in at most 7 guesses and averages 5.21 guesses. That result is from Tetsuro Tanaka (1996) and was independently confirmed by John Francis.
