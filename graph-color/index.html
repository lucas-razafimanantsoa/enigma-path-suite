How map colouring works

Imagine a map where every region gets a colour. If two regions share a border, they must look different. In a graph, the regions become nodes (dots) and the shared borders become edges (lines).

The rule

Two nodes joined by a line must never have the same colour. A dashed red line means you have broken the rule — change one of its two nodes.

The goal

Use as few colours as possible. The smallest number that can work is called the graph's chromatic number — look for the "Minimum colours" badge above.

How to play

  • Click a node to cycle its colour A → B → C → D (right-click or Shift-click to go back).
  • Watch the counters for conflicts, moves and colours used.
  • Use Check to test your answer, Reset colours to start over, or Next graph for a new puzzle.

The Four Colour Theorem

Any flat map can be coloured with at most four colours so that no two neighbouring regions share a colour. In graph language: every planar graph is 4-colourable. It was proved with computer assistance in 1976 by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken — one of the first famous proofs to rely on a computer.

Why it matters: it explains why any world map, board game or timetable can be sorted into just four conflict-free groups — a handy idea whenever things must not overlap.
